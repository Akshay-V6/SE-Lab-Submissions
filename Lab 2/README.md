## Reflection

### 1. How did planning help organize the work?

The backlog connected each customer-facing story to an epic and to the Lab 1 requirement it implements. Acceptance criteria made the expected behavior reviewable before work began. Sprint 1 grouped the selection and review flow, while Sprint 2 held the payment and confirmation work. This made the planned scope and the remaining dependency visible.

### 2. What did story-point estimation add?

Fibonacci estimates gave the team a relative sizing scale without pretending to know exact hours. The five stories were estimated at 3, 2, 3, 2, and 5 points. The larger estimate for payment reflects its extra uncertainty around approval, decline, retry, and order creation. Estimation also made the two sprint loads easy to compare: 10 points and 5 points.

### 3. How did the board workflow help?

The To Do, In Progress, and Done columns showed the state of each story and made completion visible. Moving stories across the board provided a simple view of progress and surfaced whether any work remained when a sprint was closed. All five planned stories reached Done; starter template issues were left out of the lab sprints.

### 4. What does the burndown show, and what are its limits?

The chart below summarizes the planned story points at sprint start and the remaining points after the Jira issues were marked Done: Sprint 1 went from 10 to 0 and Sprint 2 went from 5 to 0. The Jira reports page exposed a Sprint burndown chart option, but selecting it routed to a not-found page in this site. This chart therefore summarizes the captured Jira sprint totals rather than claiming to be an exported Jira report. All status transitions were performed during one lab session, so the recorded drop is concentrated at the start of each one-week window; it does not represent a realistic day-by-day work pace.
